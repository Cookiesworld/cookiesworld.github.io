I spent two days this week on a course called [Manager to Leader](https://www.qa.com/course-catalogue/courses/manager-to-leader-mpdmtl/), part of my MSc apprenticeship. I went in slightly skeptical. I've been writing software for over twenty years. Leadership frameworks felt like something for people who'd stopped being hands-on.

I was wrong.

## Day one: the hidden quadrant

We looked at how competence and clarity allow you to relinquish control safely. Creating an environment for thinking and delegating control closer to the people who are closest to the problem gives people psychological ownership. For me this is an interesting observation, I am a person who wants to be doing things rather than delegate them, yet to be successful a team of people should beat the individual.

Then we moved onto the [Johari Window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window) — a model for self-awareness that maps what you know about yourself against what others know about you. Four quadrants: open, blind, hidden, unknown.

The one that landed hardest was the hidden quadrant. The things you know about yourself that others don't. As an engineer I'm comfortable with precision and privacy. I tend to only share what's necessary. I had assumed that this was professionalism. 

The reframe was uncomfortable: when you keep things hidden, you make it harder for your team to trust you. Reducing the hidden quadrant and deliberately letting people in, isn't oversharing. It's how you build credibility and authenticity.

The session on values reinforced this. Where your values align with the organisation's, you lead with energy. Where they conflict, there's a cost. I've always quietly absorbed that friction rather than naming it. Naming it turns out to be a useful first step.

## Day two: change, silos, and bias
Day two covered more ground and was, if anything, more uncomfortable.

We looked at the Kübler-Ross change curve — originally a model for grief, now widely used to describe how people respond to organisational change. Shock, denial, frustration, depression, experiment, decision, integration. Seeing it mapped out made me think about times I've been impatient with colleagues who seemed to be resisting something obviously sensible. They weren't being difficult. They were just at a different point on the curve to me.

The session on breaking silos hit close to home. My team is split between Manchester and London developers in the north, leadership in the south. The friction that creates is real, and it's easy to explain it as geography. The course pushed back on that. Silos are as much about habits and assumptions as they are about distance. We talked about communities of practice as a way through: shared spaces where people across boundaries build common understanding rather than just reporting upward. I've seen that work. I want to be more intentional about it.

The hardest session was on unconscious bias. I consider myself analytical and evidence-driven. The point of unconscious bias is that it operates beneath that. You can't logic your way out of something you don't know you're doing. The exercise we did didn't reveal anything dramatic, but it was a useful reminder that objectivity is something you have to actively work at, not a default state.

## The bigger question
The course is designed for people moving from managing tasks to leading people. What I keep coming back to is that the gap isn't really about skills. It's about being willing to be seen; to share what you think, what you value, what you're uncertain about. To name the friction instead of absorbing it, to break down the walls: between teams, between what you know and what others need to know. In a way that technical work rarely demands, I will certainly have to push out of my comfort zone.
